Week of July 5, 2026
The World Cup Is the World's Largest City Branding Stress Test

This week, the World Cup gave us something rare in city branding: a live, real-time stress test. 

Transit agencies in Atlanta, Los Angeles, Philadelphia, and Seattle reported record ridership on match days. Millions of visitors — many using these cities' public transit for the first time — formed impressions that will stay with them for years.

Here is what that means for city branding. 

Mega-events do not create city brands. They expose them. 

The cities that invested proactively in long-term, technology-driven mobility solutions are now collecting on that investment — in front of a global audience. The cities that didn't are managing the gap between the image they projected and the reality visitors are experiencing. 

Smart technologies are not neutral. This week, they were visible. 

---

Let me walk through what that looked like in practice.

Start with Los Angeles. 

L.A. is hosting eight World Cup matches. But L.A.'s leadership has been publicly clear: this is not the goal. This is the rehearsal. The 2028 Summer Olympics is the goal.

L.A. Metro inaugurated three new underground stations just in time for the tournament — the result of six decades of planning and construction. They consolidated eight separate apps into one platform. They introduced contactless payments on buses. 

These are not temporary World Cup measures. They are permanent infrastructure decisions, delivered on a mega-event deadline, with an Olympic legacy built in.

That is the branding strategy: use one global moment to build and test the infrastructure that will define your city's identity at the next, larger one. 

But the same report that documented L.A.'s progress also documented the gap. Most bus stops remain unsheltered. The tree canopy in the neighbourhood hosting the main stadium sits at 9 percent — when the recommended standard is 30.

L.A.'s Climate Action Plan commits to net zero by 2045 and a transit-first Olympics. The distance between that commitment and today's reality is exactly where city brand credibility is won or lost.

---

Now look at Atlanta.

Atlanta launched free public WiFi this week — timed for the World Cup, but explicitly framed as long-term. Mayor Andre Dickens called it "an investment in Atlanta's future as a global centre for innovation." The initiative has a city-wide name: Integrated Atlanta. This is phase one. 

Here is the branding lesson. A temporary event measure and a permanent brand commitment are two completely different stories. 

Atlanta chose the second story. That choice — that single framing decision — is where brand strategy happens.

---

And here is my favourite story of the week — because it shows that cities are not the only ones treating the World Cup as a brand stage.

At the Brazil–Norway match in New Jersey this Sunday, Hyundai staged the World Cup's first-ever robot-powered halftime show. Atlas — the humanoid robot developed by Boston Dynamics — walked out of the player tunnel, performed goal celebrations from stars like Haaland and Kane, and delivered the match ball to the referee. In front of eighty thousand people in the stadium, and millions watching worldwide.

Hyundai's chief marketing officer explained the logic in one sentence: "Rather than talking about innovation, we wanted to demonstrate it at the biggest scale possible." 

Now, why does this matter for city branding?

Because Hyundai just demonstrated the exact playbook that host cities should be using. Don't announce your innovation. Perform it. Live, in public, at the moment of maximum attention. 

Seattle's light rail records, Atlanta's ridership highs, L.A.'s new metro stations — these are cities performing their infrastructure the same way Hyundai performed its robot. The difference is that some cities planned for this moment years in advance, and some are improvising.


So what's the takeaway this week? 

Permanent, proactive solutions strengthen brand narratives.

The World Cup transit records did not happen because of the World Cup. They happened because cities made long-term, technology-driven decisions years before the tournament — and embedded those decisions into their identity. The World Cup simply gave those decisions a global audience. 

Anticipate the global challenge. Build the long-term, technology-driven response. And embed it into your brand strategy from the very beginning. [PAUSE]

The question for every city: when the world next turns its attention to you — what will it find?
