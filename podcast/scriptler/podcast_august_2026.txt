Welcome to Smart City Branding. [PAUSE]

We are back after a summer pause, and this is our first monthly edition. [PAUSE]

Looking across the whole of August, one pattern stood out above everything else. AI stopped being a backend tool, and became a question of civic identity. [PAUSE]

Cities are no longer asking only what AI can do for them operationally. They are deciding what their relationship with AI says about who they are. [PAUSE]

Whether they want to be seen as responsible, resident-first and trustworthy, or simply fast. [PAUSE]

And here is the interesting part. As the data centre boom accelerates, some cities are courting investment at any cost. Others are drawing lines, and discovering that restraint is itself a form of positioning. [PAUSE]

Let me show you what I mean. [PAUSE]

---

Start with Chicago. [PAUSE]

Chicago already hosts thirty-nine data centres, and sits among the largest data centre markets in the country. [PAUSE]

This month, rather than chase more at any cost, Mayor Brandon Johnson signed an executive order establishing safeguards, created a task force, and urged the city council to enact a temporary moratorium. [PAUSE]

His framing was the positioning. The city is open to innovation and investment, but it must be done responsibly, transparently, and in partnership with the people most affected. [PAUSE]

And here is what makes it a real branding story, not just a policy. It is contested. Illinois' governor separately called for a two-year pause on data centre tax breaks, and a nearby project won approval anyway. Not everyone agrees restraint is the right call. [PAUSE]

That tension is the point. In the AI infrastructure rush, how a city says no is becoming as defining as how it says yes. [PAUSE]

---

Now contrast that with what happened in Gilroy, California. [PAUSE]

The Wall Street Journal reported that Amazon built a roughly two billion dollar data centre there, approved by a single city staff signature under forty-five-year-old zoning. No council vote. No public hearing. [PAUSE]

Many residents only found out once construction had already begun. [PAUSE]

Set those two cities side by side, and the lesson is clear. Transparency is itself a branding choice. [PAUSE]

A city that lets major infrastructure arrive unannounced spends trust it cannot easily rebuild. Gilroy is now rewriting its code to require public hearings for future projects. But the first impression is already made. [PAUSE]

---

The same theme runs through Europe. [PAUSE]

ICLEI Europe published a toolkit for responsible AI in urban climate action, built around real projects. Athens is using AI to detect wildfires. Barcelona is mapping heat and energy poverty. Stockholm is building a climate data platform. [PAUSE]

But the framing is what matters. These cities are not just using AI. They are demonstrating how to use it responsibly, with transparency as the starting point. [PAUSE]

In a moment when public trust in AI is fragile, a city that visibly governs its AI is building a values-based identity that pure capability cannot match. [PAUSE]

---

Now here is the story I found most striking this month. Chicago. [PAUSE]

Mayor Brandon Johnson signed an executive order establishing safeguards for data centres, created a task force, and called for a temporary moratorium. [PAUSE]

His words were the positioning. He said the city is open to innovation and investment, but it must be done responsibly, transparently, and in partnership with the people most affected. [PAUSE]

Think about what that means as a brand stance. As AI drives a global data centre rush, Chicago is choosing a clear position. [PAUSE]

A city that welcomes the technology, but refuses to let it strain water, energy or community health. That is identity through restraint. [PAUSE]

And it distinguishes Chicago from cities courting data centres at any cost. [PAUSE]

---

Two more signals worth your attention this month. [PAUSE]

In Latin America, cities reached ten thousand electric buses in operation. Eighty cities across thirteen countries, avoiding an estimated ten million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year. [PAUSE]

This is collective identity in action. Cities building a regional reputation for climate leadership together, and sharing the financing models that made it possible. [PAUSE]

And in Los Angeles, Archer and AEG announced downtown LA's first air taxi vertiport, tied directly to the 2028 Olympic Games. [PAUSE]

A company and a city co-branding a future mobility identity, using a mega-event as the launch narrative. [PAUSE]

---

So what is the takeaway from August? [PAUSE]

For most of the last decade, smart city meant visible technology. Apps, dashboards, sensors, and lately, chatbots. [PAUSE]

But in recent months something deeper has come into view. [PAUSE]

For cities, artificial intelligence is no longer about the interface. It is about the infrastructure beneath it. The data centres, the energy they draw, the water they consume, and the strain they place on communities already facing a climate crisis. [PAUSE]

This shift matters for branding because infrastructure is not neutral. [PAUSE]

A data centre is physical, visible, and sits at the intersection of competing demands. Land, power, water, jobs, and public trust. [PAUSE]

The decision to welcome one, restrain one, or quietly permit one is never only a technical choice. It is a statement about what kind of place a city wants to be, and whom it serves. [PAUSE]

That is why the same underlying technology has produced such different identities. [PAUSE]

Chicago chose responsible growth and made restraint its position. Gilroy let a project arrive almost invisibly, and spent public trust it will struggle to rebuild. Pennsylvania handed the decision to local communities. [PAUSE]

Three cities, one technology, three very different brands. [PAUSE]

And this is the lesson I keep returning to in my research. Smart technologies are not neutral infrastructures. [PAUSE]

They actively shape how a city defines itself, how it is perceived, and how it positions its future. [PAUSE]

In the age of AI, the way a city builds, and the way it decides what to build, has become one of the clearest statements it can make about its identity. [PAUSE]
